# GE Under Immelt and the Financial Arm That Nearly Destroyed an Industrial Empire: A Study in Conglomerate Risk Migration

## Background

General Electric Company was, for most of the twentieth century, one of the most admired and studied corporations in the world. Founded by Thomas Edison in 1892, GE grew into a diversified industrial conglomerate whose products ranged from jet engines and power turbines to medical imaging equipment and household appliances. Under the leadership of Jack Welch from 1981 to 2001, GE became the most valuable company in the world by market capitalization — a position it held at the turn of the millennium — and the management philosophy Welch developed became a template that business schools and executives around the world studied and emulated.

Jeff Immelt succeeded Welch as CEO in September 2001, four days before the September 11 attacks. Over the following sixteen years, he presided over a period in which GE's market capitalization fell from approximately $400 billion to approximately $150 billion. In 2017, Immelt resigned. His successor embarked on a restructuring that involved the sale of major business units, the slashing of GE's dividend for only the third time since the Great Depression, and a fundamental reassessment of what GE was and should be.

The causes of GE's decline under Immelt are multiple and debated. But the most structurally significant — the one that created the greatest systemic risk — was the growth of GE Capital, the company's financial services division, to a scale that made the entire industrial enterprise existentially dependent on the stability of a financial business it did not fully control.

## GE Capital and the Profit Machine

GE Capital was not a new creation under Immelt. The division had existed for decades and had been a significant contributor to GE's earnings under Welch. The financial services business — which encompassed commercial lending, real estate financing, consumer credit, and insurance, among other activities — generated returns that the industrial businesses could not match. Under Welch, GE Capital had grown to represent approximately 40 percent of GE's earnings.

The appeal was straightforward. Industrial businesses — manufacturing jet engines, building power turbines, producing medical imaging equipment — are capital-intensive, cyclical, and subject to long development and sales cycles. Financial services businesses, in favorable conditions, can generate high returns on capital with shorter cycles and lower fixed costs. For a conglomerate seeking to deliver consistent earnings growth to its shareholders, a large and profitable financial services arm was a powerful tool.

Under Immelt, GE Capital continued to expand. The division moved aggressively into commercial real estate lending and other structured financial products during the early-to-mid 2000s, a period of unusually favorable credit conditions and rising asset prices. GE Capital's balance sheet grew to over $600 billion in assets. The division was, by many measures, one of the largest financial institutions in the United States — comparable in scale to major commercial banks.

## The 2008 Crisis and the Exposure

The financial crisis of 2008 exposed the risk that GE Capital's scale had created for the entire GE enterprise. The crisis was generated by a collapse in the value of mortgage-backed securities and related instruments, followed by a liquidity crisis in the commercial paper markets — the short-term borrowing markets on which large financial institutions depended to fund their operations.

GE Capital, like other large financial institutions, relied heavily on commercial paper markets to fund its lending activities. When the commercial paper market seized up in the fall of 2008, GE Capital's ability to continue funding its operations was threatened. The parent company — the industrial GE — found itself in the position of potentially needing to absorb the losses and provide the liquidity support necessary to prevent GE Capital from failing.

The consequences were severe. GE's stock price fell approximately 50 percent in the weeks following Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway invested $3 billion in GE preferred stock in October 2008 — a transaction that provided needed capital but also signaled to the market that GE required external support. GE cut its dividend by 68 percent in 2009 — the first significant dividend cut since the Great Depression — to preserve capital.

GE Capital survived the crisis but at significant cost. The division's real estate portfolio required years of work-out. The losses GE absorbed were in the billions. And the near-death experience of 2008 prompted the recognition, belatedly, that GE Capital's scale had created a systemic vulnerability that was fundamentally incompatible with the identity and purpose of an industrial company.

## The Unwinding and Its Costs

Following the 2008 crisis, GE spent years attempting to reduce GE Capital's scale and risk profile. Immelt announced plans to reduce GE Capital to a smaller, more focused financial services business that supported GE's industrial operations rather than operating as an independent profit center in its own right.

The unwinding was slow, expensive, and complicated by the market conditions that followed the financial crisis. Assets that had been acquired at peak prices had to be sold into a depressed market. Regulatory scrutiny increased — GE Capital was designated as a Systemically Important Financial Institution by U.S. regulators in 2013, subjecting it to the capital requirements and oversight that applied to major banks.

By the time Immelt's successor, John Flannery, took over in 2017, GE Capital had been substantially reduced but the damage to GE's industrial businesses had accumulated. The years of earnings support from GE Capital had masked underperformance in the industrial units. The capital that had been allocated to GE Capital's expansion had not been available for the investment in research, development, and operational improvement that the industrial businesses required to remain competitive. The power business — one of GE's core industrial divisions — was facing structural challenges that inadequate investment had exacerbated.

## The Organizational Complexity

An additional dimension of GE's difficulties under Immelt was the complexity created by managing an organization that was simultaneously an industrial conglomerate and one of the largest financial institutions in the United States. The risk management frameworks, regulatory requirements, capital allocation disciplines, and talent profiles appropriate for an industrial manufacturer are fundamentally different from those appropriate for a financial services company.

Managing both within a single organizational structure — with shared leadership, shared capital allocation decisions, and shared balance sheet — created tensions and gaps that contributed to both the financial crisis exposure and the subsequent difficulty of unwinding it.

## Conclusion: The Chain

GE's decline under Immelt traces a chain that begins with the expansion of a financial services division beyond the scale appropriate for an industrial company and ends with a restructuring that required the dismantling of an institution that had been built over more than a century.

The sequence ran as follows:

GE Capital expanded aggressively into commercial real estate and structured finance during favorable credit conditions, growing to over $600 billion in assets → the scale of GE Capital created existential dependence: if the financial division encountered severe stress, the entire GE enterprise would be at risk → the 2008 financial crisis produced precisely that stress → GE Capital's commercial paper funding was threatened, forcing the parent company to provide support → the dividend was cut by 68% and external capital was required → years of earnings contributions from GE Capital had masked underperformance in the industrial businesses → the capital directed to GE Capital's expansion had not been invested in the industrial units → the industrial businesses, particularly power, faced structural challenges that insufficient investment had compounded → the unwinding of GE Capital consumed management attention and capital for years → by the time the restructuring was complete, the industrial businesses had deteriorated to a degree that required further asset sales and restructuring.

**Financial arm expanded beyond appropriate scale → industrial company became existentially dependent on a financial business → financial crisis threatened the financial arm → industrial parent absorbed the loss → capital not invested in industrial businesses → industrial units deteriorated → restructuring required selling core assets built over a century.**

The chain illustrates a risk that is specific to conglomerates but generalizes to any organization that allows one division's growth to create systemic dependency for the whole. The industrial GE did not make risky financial decisions. GE Capital did. But because GE Capital had grown large enough that its failure would threaten the parent, the risks taken in the financial division became, without explicit decision or clear visibility, the risks of the entire organization. The chain ran across divisional boundaries. The consequences did not respect them.
