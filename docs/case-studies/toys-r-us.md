# Toys R Us and the Partnership That Surrendered the Future: A Study in Outsourced Strategy

## Background

Toys R Us was founded in 1948 by Charles Lazarus in Washington D.C., initially as a baby furniture store that expanded into toys. Over the following decades, it grew into the dominant specialty toy retailer in the United States and one of the largest in the world. At its peak, Toys R Us operated over 1,500 stores globally across the Toys R Us and Babies R Us banners, generated annual revenues exceeding $11 billion, and held a position in American retail culture that was reinforced by one of the most recognizable advertising jingles in the history of consumer marketing.

Toys R Us filed for bankruptcy in September 2017. By June 2018, it had announced the closure of all remaining U.S. stores — a complete liquidation of a business that had defined its category for half a century.

The story of Toys R Us is frequently told as a story of private equity — the company was taken private in a leveraged buyout in 2005 that saddled it with approximately $5 billion in debt, leaving insufficient capital for the investment in stores and e-commerce that the competitive environment required. This narrative is accurate as far as it goes. But the decision that made Toys R Us's competitive position fundamentally untenable was made five years before the leveraged buyout, in 2000, and it was a technology decision that appeared at the time to be a reasonable response to operational constraints.

## The Amazon Deal

In 2000, as the commercial internet was transforming retail, Toys R Us faced the challenge that confronted every major brick-and-mortar retailer of that era: how to establish an e-commerce presence quickly enough to serve customers who were beginning to expect the ability to shop online.

Building e-commerce capability from scratch required investment in technology infrastructure, fulfillment operations, and digital marketing expertise that Toys R Us did not have and would take years to develop internally. Amazon, which had launched in 1994 as a bookseller and was in the process of expanding into a broad e-commerce platform, offered a solution: Toys R Us would become the exclusive toy seller on Amazon's platform, with Amazon handling the fulfillment and technology operations in exchange for fees.

The deal was announced in 2000. From Toys R Us's perspective, it solved an immediate problem efficiently — it established an online presence without requiring the company to build the underlying infrastructure. From Amazon's perspective, it provided a major brand partner and assured toy inventory during a critical phase of the company's expansion beyond books.

The partnership began to deteriorate almost immediately. Toys R Us discovered that Amazon, despite the exclusivity provisions of the agreement, was allowing third-party sellers to offer toys on the Amazon platform — a direct violation, in Toys R Us's view, of the exclusivity terms. Toys R Us filed suit against Amazon in 2004, alleging breach of contract. A New Jersey court ruled in Toys R Us's favor in 2006, freeing the company to terminate the partnership.

## What Amazon Had Built in the Meantime

By the time Toys R Us reclaimed its freedom to build its own e-commerce operations in 2006, Amazon had spent six years building one of the most powerful toy categories in e-commerce. The third-party sellers that Amazon had allowed onto its platform — in violation of the exclusivity agreement — had created a vast, deeply competitive toy marketplace that served customers with selection, price transparency, and fulfillment speed that no single-brand retailer could match.

The period from 2000 to 2006, during which Toys R Us had outsourced its e-commerce operations to Amazon, was precisely the period in which e-commerce consumer behavior was being formed. The customers who learned to buy online during those years — who discovered the convenience of ordering at home, comparing prices across sellers, and receiving purchases by mail — formed habits and preferences that were difficult to change. Amazon's early investment in customer experience, logistics, and platform breadth during this period established the behavioral patterns and the infrastructure that would define e-commerce for the following two decades.

When Toys R Us attempted to build its own e-commerce platform after 2006, it was doing so against a competitor that had six years of head start, a vastly superior logistics network, a larger selection, more competitive pricing, and entrenched consumer habits. The internal e-commerce platform Toys R Us eventually built was functional but never competitive. It consistently lost to Amazon on the dimensions that mattered most to online shoppers.

## The Leveraged Buyout and the Capital Constraint

In 2005, a consortium of private equity firms — Kohlberg Kravis Roberts, Vornado Realty Trust, and Bain Capital — acquired Toys R Us in a leveraged buyout valued at approximately $6.6 billion, of which approximately $5 billion was debt placed on the company's balance sheet.

The debt service obligations created by this structure consumed a significant portion of the company's operating cash flow annually. In the years following the buyout, Toys R Us paid hundreds of millions of dollars per year in interest expenses — capital that, in a less leveraged structure, would have been available for investment in stores, technology, and e-commerce.

The combination of the debt burden and the e-commerce disadvantage created a situation in which Toys R Us was competing against Amazon with insufficient capital and a structural technology deficit that had been created, in part, by the Amazon partnership itself.

## The Physical Retail Dimension

The debt burden also constrained Toys R Us's ability to invest in its physical stores at a time when the retail environment was becoming more demanding of investment. Competitors who were not carrying equivalent debt loads — Target, Walmart — were investing in store renovations, improved shopping experiences, and integrated online-to-offline capabilities. Toys R Us stores, which had already been criticized for a shopping experience that was dated relative to competitors, fell further behind.

The toy category, unlike some other retail categories, had not been fully commoditized. The experience of browsing toys, of seeing products on shelves, of allowing children to engage with the merchandise, retained value for a portion of the customer base. But capitalizing on this advantage required investment in the store environment that the company's debt structure made difficult to sustain.

## Conclusion: The Chain

Toys R Us's collapse traces a chain that begins with a single operational decision — outsourcing e-commerce to Amazon — and ends with the complete liquidation of a fifty-year-old retail institution, a chain in which the ceding of one capability enabled a competitor to build the infrastructure that would eventually make the entire category uncompetitive.

The sequence ran as follows:

Toys R Us outsourced its e-commerce operations to Amazon in 2000, prioritizing speed of online presence over control of the capability → Amazon violated exclusivity terms, building a toy marketplace far beyond what the partnership contemplated → during six years of the Amazon partnership, consumer e-commerce behavior was formed around Amazon's platform → Toys R Us reclaimed its e-commerce freedom in 2006 but was now competing against a vastly superior infrastructure it had allowed Amazon to build → the leveraged buyout in 2005 added $5 billion in debt, constraining investment in e-commerce and physical stores → physical stores deteriorated relative to better-capitalized competitors → e-commerce operations remained structurally inferior to Amazon → consumers migrated to Amazon and Walmart for toy purchases → revenues declined → debt service became unsustainable relative to declining cash flows → bankruptcy in 2017 → full liquidation in 2018.

**E-commerce outsourced to competitor → competitor built category dominance during partnership → reclaiming capability was too late → debt constrained catch-up investment → stores and digital both fell behind → revenue migrated to competitors → debt unsustainable → liquidation.**

The chain illustrates a category of strategic failure that is distinct from technological blindness or competitive complacency. Toys R Us was aware of the e-commerce opportunity. It made a decision — outsource rather than build — that appeared operationally rational in 2000. The decision ceded the development of a capability to a company that used it to make Toys R Us permanently uncompetitive. The damage was done during the partnership, not after it ended.
