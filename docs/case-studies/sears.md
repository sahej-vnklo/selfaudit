# Sears and the Death Spiral of Margin Optimization: A Study in Cross-Functional Collapse

## Background

Sears, Roebuck and Company was for most of the twentieth century the largest retailer in the United States and one of the most recognizable commercial institutions in American life. Founded in 1886 as a mail-order catalog business, Sears grew into a retail empire that at its peak operated over 3,500 stores, employed 300,000 people, and served as the primary shopping destination for a large portion of the American middle class. The Sears catalog, distributed to tens of millions of households, was so comprehensive that customers could order everything from clothing and appliances to prefabricated houses.

Sears invented several categories of American retail. It launched private-label brands — Kenmore appliances, Craftsman tools, DieHard batteries — that became trusted household names in their own right. It pioneered the concept of the mall anchor store. For decades, it was not merely a retailer but an institution.

By October 2018, Sears Holdings filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. At the time of filing, the company that had once been the largest retailer in America had fewer than 700 stores remaining and was hemorrhaging cash at a rate that made survival without restructuring impossible. The bankruptcy was not a surprise. What is instructive is the mechanism by which it happened — a sequence of individually defensible decisions that accumulated into a compounding, self-reinforcing collapse.

## The Lampert Era and the Financial Logic

In 2005, hedge fund manager Edward Lampert orchestrated the merger of Sears and Kmart, creating Sears Holdings. Lampert, who became chairman and eventually CEO, brought to the company a financial philosophy shaped by his background in investment management. His approach was grounded in capital allocation theory: that a business should be evaluated by its return on invested capital, that underperforming assets should be divested, and that cash generated by the business should be returned to shareholders rather than reinvested in operations that produced inadequate returns.

This philosophy was not unreasonable in the abstract. Applied to a struggling retailer, however, it produced a specific set of decisions whose individual logic obscured their collective effect.

## The Cost-Cutting Cascade

The most consequential pattern in Sears's decline was the systematic reduction of investment in the physical store experience — the stores themselves, the merchandise presentation, the staffing levels, the maintenance of the physical facilities.

The financial logic was straightforward: stores were expensive to maintain. Labor was the largest variable cost. Reducing headcount and deferring maintenance improved short-term margins. Each individual decision to reduce investment in a particular store or category appeared, in isolation, as a rational response to underperformance.

What the financial model did not adequately capture was the relationship between the quality of the store experience and the willingness of customers to shop there. Retail is a business in which the physical environment is not merely a cost center — it is the product. A store that is clean, well-lit, well-staffed, and well-merchandised attracts customers. A store that is poorly maintained, understaffed, and difficult to navigate repels them.

As Sears reduced investment in its stores, the shopping experience degraded. Fixtures went unrepaired. Shelves were understocked. Customer service became difficult to access. The physical environment communicated neglect, and customers responded to that communication by going elsewhere.

## The Supplier Relationship Deterioration

The reduction in store traffic had consequences that extended well beyond the immediate loss of sales. Sears's relationships with its merchandise suppliers were governed by terms that reflected the company's historical buying power. As a major retailer with high traffic, Sears could negotiate favorable terms — extended payment periods, cooperative advertising support, priority on new product launches.

As store traffic declined and sales volumes fell, Sears's leverage in supplier negotiations weakened. Suppliers who had previously competed to be on Sears's shelves became less eager to prioritize the chain. Some suppliers shifted their best products and marketing support toward retailers with growing traffic — Walmart, Target, Amazon. Sears found itself, in an increasing number of categories, receiving less favorable merchandise terms than its competitors, which further reduced the quality and appeal of its product assortment.

This created a reinforcing loop: declining traffic led to weaker supplier terms, which led to a less compelling merchandise assortment, which led to further declines in traffic.

## The Price Consequence

As supplier terms worsened and the cost base remained relatively fixed, Sears faced pressure on its ability to price competitively. The company that had once competed on value found itself in a position where its cost structure prevented it from matching the prices offered by competitors who had either lower cost structures (Walmart) or stronger supplier relationships (Target) or no physical stores at all (Amazon).

The private-label brands — Kenmore, Craftsman, DieHard — which had for decades been sources of differentiation and customer loyalty, were increasingly available through other retailers. The exclusive advantage these brands had provided was eroding. Customers who had once come to Sears specifically for a Kenmore appliance or a Craftsman tool could now find equivalent or superior options elsewhere, often at lower prices.

## The Structural Fragmentation

Lampert's management philosophy included an experiment in internal organizational structure that proved particularly damaging. He divided Sears Holdings into approximately 30 autonomous business units — each responsible for its own profit and loss, each competing with the others for internal resources. The theory was that internal competition and clear accountability would drive efficiency.

In practice, the structure created a fragmented organization in which different business units prioritized their own financial metrics at the expense of the customer experience. A customer entering a Sears store encountered an environment shaped by decisions made by multiple competing internal entities, each optimizing for their own metrics, with no single unit responsible for the coherent experience of the store as a whole.

The fragmentation made it structurally difficult for the company to respond to competitive threats that required coordinated action across business units. By the time a threat was identified, understood, and responded to through the internal structure, the competitive environment had moved further.

## Conclusion: The Chain

Sears's collapse traces a chain that began with a set of financial optimization decisions that were individually defensible but collectively self-reinforcing — a death spiral in which each step made the next step more likely and more severe.

The sequence ran as follows:

Investment in store maintenance and staffing was reduced to improve short-term margins → the quality of the physical shopping experience degraded → customer traffic declined → supplier leverage weakened as sales volumes fell → merchandise terms worsened → product assortment quality declined → prices became less competitive → traffic declined further → revenues fell → further cost cuts became necessary → the physical environment degraded further → the cycle accelerated.

**Finance cut costs → ops degraded → customer experience declined → traffic fell → supplier power weakened → assortment worsened → prices rose relative to competitors → traffic fell further → revenues collapsed → no investment left to recover.**

The chain is instructive because it demonstrates how a decision made entirely within the finance function — reduce operating costs to improve margins — can travel through operations, customer experience, supplier relationships, and pricing to produce a revenue collapse that appears to originate in sales or competitive dynamics. Those who diagnosed Sears's problem as a "retail problem" or an "Amazon problem" were not wrong that those pressures existed. They were wrong in identifying them as the primary cause. The primary cause was a financial philosophy that optimized one part of the system without modeling how that optimization would affect every other part.

By the time the chain became visible as a revenue and competitive problem, it had already been running for years as a cost and experience problem.
